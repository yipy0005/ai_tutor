# GREAT Framework for Problem Solving and Diagnosis

The **GREAT framework** is a simple five-stage method for approaching maths, science, reasoning, and other structured problems.

Its purpose is twofold:

1. Give the student a consistent thought process to use whenever they face a question.
2. Help a tutor identify exactly **where the student's thinking breaks down**.

The framework is:

\[
\boxed{\text{G → R → E → A → T}}
\]

---

# G — GIVEN

## Core question

**What do I know?**

The student identifies and extracts the relevant information provided by the question.

This may include:

- numbers
- measurements
- equations
- diagrams
- conditions
- facts
- observations
- relationships
- units
- keywords

### Example

> A bicycle costs £240 and is reduced by 15%.

The GIVEN information is:

\[
£240
\]

and

\[
15\%\text{ reduction}
\]

## What G measures

G tests:

- reading comprehension
- mathematical/scientific vocabulary
- attention to detail
- ability to distinguish relevant from irrelevant information
- ability to convert words into useful information

## Signs of weakness

A student may:

- miss important information
- misunderstand terminology
- copy numbers incorrectly
- overlook units
- treat irrelevant information as important
- struggle to convert prose into mathematical information

---

# R — REQUIRED

## Core question

**What am I trying to find?**

The student identifies the precise target of the question.

A useful prompt is:

> **What should my final answer represent?**

### Example

> A bicycle costs £240 and is reduced by 15%. Find the sale price.

The REQUIRED quantity is:

\[
\boxed{\text{new sale price}}
\]

not merely "15%" or "£240".

## What R measures

R tests:

- understanding of the question
- ability to identify the goal
- precision
- ability to distinguish information from the required outcome

## Signs of weakness

A student may:

- calculate something that was not asked for
- stop at an intermediate answer
- misunderstand command words
- confuse the starting quantity with the required quantity

A useful student sentence is:

> **“I am trying to find ______.”**

---

# E — EQUATION / EXPLANATION / LINK

## Core question

**What connects what I know to what I need?**

This is usually the most important reasoning stage.

In mathematics, E might be:

- an equation
- formula
- theorem
- mathematical relationship
- pattern
- diagram
- proportional relationship
- suitable representation

In science, E might be:

- a scientific principle
- mechanism
- model
- causal relationship
- hypothesis
- equation

## Example

Given:

\[
£240,\quad15\%\text{ reduction}
\]

Required:

sale price.

The link is:

\[
100\%-15\%=85\%
\]

therefore:

\[
\text{sale price}=0.85\times240
\]

## What E measures

E tests:

- conceptual understanding
- pattern recognition
- method selection
- reasoning
- ability to transfer knowledge
- mathematical/scientific intuition

This is often what separates:

> **“I know how to perform the method”**

from:

> **“I know when and why to use the method.”**

## E can be split into two subskills

### E1 — Recognition

**What tool or concept applies?**

For example:

\[
\text{right-angled triangle}\rightarrow\text{Pythagoras}
\]

### E2 — Understanding

**Why does that relationship apply here?**

A student who memorises:

> "Right triangle = Pythagoras"

may have strong E1 but weak E2.

## Signs of weakness

A student may:

- know procedures but not know when to use them
- wait for the question to name the method
- perform random calculations
- fail when familiar content is presented in an unfamiliar format
- struggle with multi-step questions

---

# A — ACT

## Core question

**Can I correctly carry out the method?**

Once the student has identified the correct relationship, they execute the mathematics or reasoning.

This includes:

- arithmetic
- algebra
- substitution
- rearranging equations
- applying formulae
- carrying out logical steps
- using diagrams
- interpreting tables
- calculating accurately

### Example

\[
0.85\times240=204
\]

Therefore:

\[
£204
\]

## What A measures

A tests:

- procedural fluency
- arithmetic accuracy
- algebraic fluency
- recall of basic facts
- working-memory efficiency
- execution speed

## Signs of weakness

A student may:

- select the correct method but calculate incorrectly
- make sign errors
- make fraction errors
- misuse a calculator
- forget multiplication facts
- lose track during multi-step manipulation

A useful rule is:

> **One meaningful transformation per line.**

---

# T — TEST

## Core question

**Does my answer work and make sense?**

The student evaluates the result instead of automatically accepting it.

They should ask:

1. Does it satisfy the original information?
2. Is it reasonable?
3. Are the units correct?
4. Did I answer the actual question?
5. Can I verify it another way?

### Example

Sale price:

\[
£204
\]

Check the discount:

\[
15\%\text{ of }240=36
\]

and:

\[
240-36=204
\]

So the answer is consistent.

## What T measures

T tests:

- estimation
- mathematical sense
- self-correction
- critical thinking
- evaluation
- confidence in challenging one's own answer

## Signs of weakness

A student may:

- accept obviously impossible answers
- forget units
- fail to notice calculation mistakes
- never substitute answers back
- assume calculator output must be correct

---

# The GREAT Diagnostic Scale

Score every stage using:

\[
\boxed{0,\ 1,\ 2}
\]

## Score 0 — Cannot do independently

The student:

- cannot complete the stage
- does not know what to do
- requires the tutor to supply the answer or method

Interpretation:

\[
\boxed{0=\text{Skill not secure}}
\]

---

## Score 1 — Can do with prompting

The student:

- partially understands
- can continue after a hint
- recognises the idea when guided
- requires questions or scaffolding

Interpretation:

\[
\boxed{1=\text{Developing}}
\]

---

## Score 2 — Independent

The student:

- performs the stage correctly without help
- can explain their reasoning
- recognises the required method independently

Interpretation:

\[
\boxed{2=\text{Secure}}
\]

---

# GREAT Scoring Table

| Stage | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G — Given** | Cannot identify relevant information | Identifies information after prompting | Extracts relevant information independently |
| **R — Required** | Does not understand the target | Identifies target with prompting | States precisely what must be found |
| **E — Link** | Cannot identify appropriate relationship/method | Recognises relationship after hint | Independently identifies and explains the link |
| **A — Act** | Cannot execute method | Executes with help or makes significant errors | Executes accurately and independently |
| **T — Test** | Does not check or recognise unreasonable results | Can check when prompted | Independently verifies and evaluates answer |

Maximum score:

\[
\boxed{10/10}
\]

But the **pattern of scores is more useful than the total**.

---

# Example Diagnostic Profile

Suppose a student scores:

\[
G=2,\quad R=2,\quad E=0,\quad A=2,\quad T=1
\]

Total:

\[
7/10
\]

The important conclusion is not simply:

> "The student scored 70%."

The useful diagnosis is:

> **The student understands questions and has good technical ability, but struggles to identify which mathematical relationship to use independently.**

The intervention should therefore focus on:

- method recognition
- comparing problem types
- identifying clues
- explaining why a method applies
- unfamiliar problems

Not more arithmetic drills.

---

# Common GREAT Profiles

## Profile 1

\[
2-2-0-2-1
\]

### Interpretation

Strong comprehension and execution.

Weak method recognition.

### Training

Prioritise:

- puzzles
- unfamiliar problems
- "Which method and why?"
- hiding the name of the technique
- clue → method associations

---

## Profile 2

\[
2-2-2-0-1
\]

### Interpretation

Strong conceptual understanding but weak technical fluency.

### Training

Prioritise:

- arithmetic drills
- times tables
- fractions
- algebraic manipulation
- procedural repetition
- accuracy

---

## Profile 3

\[
0-1-2-2-1
\]

### Interpretation

The student may know mathematics well but struggles to understand written questions.

### Training

Prioritise:

- comprehension
- mathematical vocabulary
- identifying relevant information
- turning words into diagrams
- restating questions

---

## Profile 4

\[
2-2-2-2-0
\]

### Interpretation

Strong student who makes avoidable mistakes because they do not verify answers.

### Training

Prioritise:

- estimation
- substitution
- unit checking
- error spotting
- deliberately incorrect worked examples

---

# Using GREAT During Tutoring

Do not immediately ask:

> “Can you solve this?”

Instead, sometimes conduct a **GREAT interview**.

Ask:

### G

> “What information have we been given?”

### R

> “What exactly are we trying to find?”

### E

> “What relationship could connect those?”

### A

> “Show me how you would carry it out.”

### T

> “How could you convince me your answer is correct?”

Record the **first stage where the student needs help**.

That point is often the real bottleneck.

---

# The Hint Ladder

When a student becomes stuck, do not immediately show the method.

Increase assistance gradually.

### Hint 1

Ask a GREAT question:

> “What are we trying to find?”

### Hint 2

Direct attention:

> “Which information looks important?”

### Hint 3

Prompt the concept:

> “Have we seen a relationship involving these quantities?”

### Hint 4

Name the technique:

> “Could Pythagoras help?”

### Hint 5

Demonstrate the first step.

The long-term goal is:

\[
5\rightarrow4\rightarrow3\rightarrow2\rightarrow1\rightarrow0
\]

The student gradually needs less assistance.

---

# GREAT and Question Difficulty

Questions become more difficult when parts of GREAT are hidden.

## Easy question

The question provides:

\[
G+R+E
\]

The student mainly performs:

\[
A
\]

Example:

> Use Pythagoras to calculate the missing side.

The method is supplied.

---

## Harder question

The question provides:

\[
G+R
\]

but hides:

\[
E
\]

The student must recognise Pythagoras independently.

---

## Very difficult question

The student may have to construct several links:

\[
G
\rightarrow E_1
\rightarrow\text{new information}
\rightarrow E_2
\rightarrow\text{new information}
\rightarrow E_3
\rightarrow R
\]

This explains why difficult questions often consist of relatively simple calculations connected by a hidden route.

Therefore, when a student is stuck:

\[
\boxed{\text{Ask: “Which part of GREAT is hidden from me?”}}
\]

---

# GREAT for Mathematics

For maths:

### G

What numbers, relationships, diagrams and conditions are given?

### R

What quantity must I find?

### E

What equation, formula, theorem or relationship connects them?

### A

Perform the mathematics.

### T

Check by substitution, estimation, inverse operation, units or another method.

---

# GREAT for Science

For science, redefine E slightly:

\[
\boxed{E=\text{Equation OR Explanation}}
\]

### G

What observations, conditions, variables or data are given?

### R

What must I calculate, explain, predict or evaluate?

### E

What scientific principle, mechanism, model or equation explains it?

### A

Apply the science logically.

### T

Does the evidence support the conclusion? Are the units, assumptions and explanation sensible?

This makes GREAT transferable across:

- physics
- chemistry
- biology
- experiments
- data interpretation

---

# GREAT for Selective / Grammar-School Reasoning

The framework can also describe the abilities needed in selective testing.

### G — Fast comprehension

Extract information quickly and accurately.

### R — Precise targeting

Understand exactly what is being asked.

### E — Reasoning

Identify patterns, rules and hidden relationships.

### A — Fluency

Execute basic maths or language operations quickly and accurately.

### T — Self-correction

Reject implausible answers and catch mistakes.

For selective exams, particularly important is:

\[
\boxed{E}
\]

because harder questions often deliberately hide the method.

However:

\[
\boxed{E\text{ cannot compensate indefinitely for weak }A}
\]

A student who understands everything but calculates slowly may struggle under time pressure.

Therefore:

\[
\boxed{\text{Fluency + reasoning}}
\]

must be developed together.

---

# GREAT as a Learning Progression

The student ideally progresses through:

### Stage 1 — Told the method

\[
G+R+E\rightarrow A
\]

### Stage 2 — Recognises the method

\[
G+R\rightarrow E\rightarrow A
\]

### Stage 3 — Solves multi-step problems

\[
G\rightarrow E_1\rightarrow E_2\rightarrow R
\]

### Stage 4 — Reasons independently

The student constructs their own route.

### Stage 5 — Evaluates

The student can explain:

- why their method works
- whether the answer is reasonable
- whether another method could work

---

# The Most Important Tutoring Principle

Do not diagnose students merely by saying:

> “They are weak at fractions.”

or:

> “They got Pythagoras wrong.”

Instead ask:

\[
\boxed{\text{Where did their thinking break?}}
\]

For example, two students can fail exactly the same Pythagoras question.

Student A cannot recognise that Pythagoras applies:

\[
E=0
\]

but can solve:

\[
x^2+16=25
\]

easily.

Student B immediately recognises Pythagoras:

\[
E=2
\]

but cannot manipulate:

\[
x^2+16=25.
\]

Therefore:

\[
A=0.
\]

They need completely different teaching.

---

# The GREAT Master Question

Ultimately, train the student to internalise:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{array}{c}
\textbf{G — What do I know?}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\
\textbf{R — What do I need?}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\
\textbf{E — What connects them?}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\
\textbf{A — What should I do?}\\[4pt]
\downarrow\\
\textbf{T — Does it work?}
\end{array}
}
\]

When stuck, the student should not think:

> “I can't do this.”

They should think:

> **“Which part of GREAT am I stuck on?”**

That turns confusion into a diagnosable problem.

---

# One-Line Summary

\[
\boxed{
\textbf{GREAT =
Understand the information →
identify the goal →
find the relationship →
execute →
verify}
}
\]

And the diagnostic scale is:

\[
\boxed{
0=\text{Cannot yet}
\qquad
1=\text{With help}
\qquad
2=\text{Independent}
}
\]

The objective is not simply to produce more correct answers.

The objective is to progressively move every stage from:

\[
\boxed{0\rightarrow1\rightarrow2}
\]

until the entire GREAT process becomes an automatic habit of thought.
